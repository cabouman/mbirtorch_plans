Flash remediation — Phase 2c: the split_sino_recon seam

What causes the seam stripes in split reconstructions of real data, and which interventions fix them · started 2026-07-08 · overview and other pages: index.html · the axial and radial cases: phase_2a_axial_results.html, phase_2b_radial_results.html · plan of record: flash_remediation_plan.md · scripts: archive/flash_remediation/experiments/

TL;DR. split_sino_recon halves the memory of a cone-beam reconstruction by reconstructing the detector-row halves separately and stitching; each half keeps hsino sinogram rows past the split and reconstructs hrecon slices past it (both 5 by default). On real data (the Lilly D01788 scan) the split without its sine taper produces structural ±40% stripes within ~10 slices of the seam. The cause has two parts, both geometric. Each half's kept sinogram rows see hsino·(1+R/SID)·pitch slices past the split but its recon extends only hrecon — Phase 2a's axial truncation in miniature, setting the scale of the trouble — and the load-bearing driver is a sub-row misalignment between the sinogram cut row and the recon split slice (~0.4 rows on Lilly): with the two exactly aligned, the same truncation depth is benign. The artifact needs no object structure and no data imperfection — it is fully reproduced synthetically (§4) with a steep dose-response in the misalignment — and it hides from ordinary phantom studies because default synthetic models lock the two grids together. Of the interventions, the shipped sine taper suppresses the stripes at 4× downsampling (11×) but fails at 8× (7.9×10−3 → only 6.1×10−3); the geometry-derived deeper extension works in both regimes (~9×10−4 at 8×, decaying with iterations — normal convergence restored), with the formula depth sufficient; and exact cut–split alignment removes the driver outright (to the numerical floor in the ablation, at zero memory cost). Proposals (plan only, no code yet): set hrecon = ⌈hsino·(1+R/SID)·pitch⌉ + a small PSF margin, and/or an opt-in sub-slice grid shift that aligns the cut and the split (the mismatch is invariant under index choices — see the remedies page).

1  The problem: seam stripes on real data

split_sino_recon reduces peak memory by roughly 2×: it splits the detector rows at the row nearest the iso plane, reconstructs each half against its own slab of slices, and stitches the two half-volumes at the split. Two overlap depths define the design. Each half keeps hsino sinogram rows past the iso row (the sino overlap) and reconstructs hrecon slices past the split (the recon overlap, used during the half's reconstruction and discarded at stitching); the shipped defaults are hsino = hrecon = 5, and the shipped design also multiplies the overlap rows' weights by a quarter-sine taper falling to zero. Phase 2c asks which of these elements matter.

The exhibit below is the reason this question is not academic. It is the Lilly D01788 NSI scan (4× detector downsampling, 2× view subsampling, 900 views, transmission_root weights, magnification 4.69, R/SID = 0.21), reconstructed with the split as it stood on 2026-06-26 — before the taper existed — against the same sinogram reconstructed unsplit at the same 15 iterations:

Two facts sharpen the problem. The stripes are structural, not a convergence transient: at 60 iterations the background converges 5× further while the seam error persists undiminished (§3). And they are not specific to a library version: the reconstruction above used the 2026-06-26 code, but re-running the same no-taper split on the current branch — whose projector internals changed substantially in between — reproduces the stripes at full strength (peak per-slice seam RMS 7.3×10−3 vs ~8×10−3). In fact §4 shows they are not a property of the measured data at all: the stripes come from the scan geometry and the split design.

2  The cause: a misaligned cut at the halves' extension ends

Two structural facts frame the split, worked out in the plan's mechanism discussion. First, the data term is almost separable at the split: with a circular orbit the source sits in the iso plane, so a ray to a detector row above iso never sees material below the iso plane, and a voxel above the plane never projects onto rows below iso. Second, the qGGMRF prior is not separable: neighboring slices couple, so each half's extended slices act as prior boundary conditions for the kept slices — which is why the halves reconstruct overlap slices at all, and why those slices must be data-accurate. The sino overlap exists to feed them.

The split design — side view

The raw material for the failure sits at the extension ends. Each half keeps hsino overlap rows past the iso row (orange bracket) and reconstructs hrecon overlap slices past the split (dashed bands). But the outermost overlap rows see material beyond the extension (red bands): a detector row hsino rows past iso collects rays from slices out to hsino·(1+R/SID)·pitch past the split at the far edge of the field of view.

That geometric excess is the first half of the cause. The half cannot place attenuation where its recon has no voxels, so — exactly as in Phase 2a's axial truncation, where unexplained attenuation piles up at the top of the slab as a flash — it dumps the excess at the extension's end. Here the unexplained depth is hsino·(1+R/SID)·pitch − hrecon = 5·1.21 − 5 ≈ 1.1 slices on Lilly — and unlike Phase 2a's flash at a far physical boundary, this one sits a few slices from the seam, where the prior coupling and the stitch blend carry the inconsistency straight into the kept slices.

The synthetic hunt (§4) later sharpened this mechanism in an important way: the truncation depth alone is not what makes the seam visible. With the sinogram cut row and the recon split slice exactly aligned, the two halves suffer the same end deficit symmetrically and the seam stays at the numerical floor even at Lilly's fan angle and the default hrecon = 5. The load-bearing driver is the sub-row relative misalignment between the cut and the split — on Lilly the cut row sits 0.05 rows from iso while the split slice sits 0.45 slices from it, a ~0.4-row mismatch (the detector-row and slice grids are positioned independently in a real scan; a default synthetic model locks them together, which is why the effect hides from ordinary phantom studies). The misalignment breaks the symmetry between the halves' end deficits, and their disagreement lands at the seam as the alternating pattern; the response rises steeply — superlinearly — with the mismatch, and needs no object structure at all (§4). The deep extension still works because its margin covers the deficit including the misalignment's contribution.

3  The interventions, on the real data

The interventions tested here follow from the truncation side of the mechanism: down-weight the overlap rows so the model is not forced to explain what it cannot represent (the shipped sine taper — treating the symptom), or extend the recon overlap to cover everything the kept rows see (a geometry-derived hrecon — covering the shortfall). A third intervention — aligning the cut with the split, which removes the driver itself — emerged only from §4's root-cause hunt and appears in the proposals below. All variants below use the same parameterized split (hsino = 5, real transmission_root weights, fixed iteration counts) and are judged against the matching-iteration unsplit reconstruction:

The regime probe: 8× downsampling separates the two fixes

To get a faster experimental turnaround we re-ran the study at 8× detector downsampling and 8× view subsampling (225 views, 235×187 detector). The geometry that drives the artifact comes along at any downsampling — R/SID is physical, the pitch ratio stays ~1 (unexplained depth still ~1 slice), and the scan's own offsets keep the cut–split mismatch large (~0.3 rows at 4×, ~0.4 at 8×) — and indeed the stripes persist at full strength. But the regime change separates the interventions:

The asymmetry is the story. The taper is a suppressor: how well down-weighting the inconsistent rows works depends on everything else in the balance — here the view count dropped 4× and the per-bin statistics changed, and the taper's effectiveness collapsed from 11× to 1.3×. The matched extension covers the shortfall itself, and its performance carries across regimes (and the depth beyond the formula buys nothing, exactly as covering-the-shortfall predicts). Practically this also means the shipped split produces visible seam stripes at coarse downsampling — settings users actually run for quick looks.

The proposals (plan only — no code yet)

Two candidates, complementary. Set the recon overlap from geometry: hrecon = ⌈hsino·(1+R/SID)·pitch⌉ + a small PSF margin (which also absorbs the worst-case half-slice mismatch), so every slice the kept sinogram rows can see is representable — a few extra slices per half, fixes both regimes above. And, from the §4 discovery, align the split with the cut. In the consistent-data ablation, exact alignment alone took the Lilly seam from 8.5×10−3 to 1.1×10−5 at the default hrecon = 5 — better than the deep extension achieved, with no extra slices. One honest wrinkle, analyzed in the remedies page (2d): at the default pitch ratio the row and slice grids are commensurate, so the mismatch is invariant under any choice of cut row or split slice — alignment is only reachable by an opt-in sub-slice shift of the reconstruction grid itself, which moves the output sampling positions relative to recon(). Belt-and-suspenders would do both, and the taper then becomes unnecessary in every regime tested; whether to retain it is a call for implementation time. This is the campaign's recurring theme: give the model the support (and the geometry) to explain the data rather than down-weighting the data. One caveat carries over from the mechanism analysis: all of this rests on the circular orbit (source in the iso plane); if the zero-helical-shift restriction is ever relaxed, the separability breaks and the design needs re-testing.

4  What the synthetic study adds — and where it misled

Phase 2c began as a synthetic A/B (a laminate phantom crossing the split plus a smooth control, fully inside the field of view so all seam error is split-induced; four variants against an unsplit reference). Its durable result concerns the other design element, the sino overlap itself:

But the same synthetic initially misled on the taper question: with the overlap at full weight, the no-taper split matched the unsplit reference to the float-noise floor (2–3×10−5), and we briefly proposed removing the taper outright. The stripes then resisted eleven build-up attempts at Lilly's own fan angle: high-contrast solid pins crossing the seam, transmission_root weight texture, photon noise, axially segmented pins matching the real object's 1–3 slice chains, and detector-row binning — where emulating the binning linearly turned out to be provably inert (averaging line integrals is exactly the projection of an axially smoothed object, still perfectly consistent data), and binning in transmission space, as real preprocessing does, injects a Jensen-gap inconsistency that was simply too weak at this contrast.

Closing the gap: ablate the real data instead

With the build-up direction exhausted, the hunt reversed: start from the striped real case and remove ingredients until the stripes die. Three ablations on the exact Lilly 8× problem, each changing one thing: replace the real sinogram with the forward projection of the reference recon — perfectly model-consistent data, same object, same geometry — and the stripes persist (7.7×10−3, 298× background): inconsistency ruled out. Set the weights to unit — they persist (8.5×10−3): weights ruled out. Zero the axial offsets so the cut and the split are exactly aligned — they vanish (1.1×10−5, a 740× drop). The driver is the sub-row misalignment between the cut and the split (§2). The forward confirmation then closed the loop: dialing det_row_offset into the plain synthetic — the only change from conditions that were clean — produces the stripes with a steep dose-response, on a completely smooth body as strongly as on structured phantoms, with the every-other-slice zigzag signature and the real Lilly point falling on the synthetic curve:

The methodological lesson sharpened twice along the way. Our first explanation of the original synthetic's false verdict — that its narrow fan angle kept the truncation depth below threshold — was itself wrong: the wide-fan replication at Lilly's R/SID stayed just as clean. The true blind spot is that a default synthetic model cannot exhibit this artifact at all, because it places the detector-row and slice grids symmetrically — the cut and the split come out mutually aligned, canceling the driver exactly (even an apparent 0.5-slice offset case had both grids shifted together, relative mismatch zero). Real scans position the two grids independently. The lesson, final form: a synthetic validation inherits its geometry's blind spots — stress conclusions at the real datasets' governing ratios, and with the real datasets' broken symmetries.
